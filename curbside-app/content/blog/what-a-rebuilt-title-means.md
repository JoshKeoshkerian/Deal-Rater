## Introduction

You find a car you like on Facebook Marketplace. The photos look good, the mileage is reasonable, and the price is well below similar cars. Then you read the fine print in the listing: rebuilt title. Or you ask the seller for the title and see the word rebuilt stamped on it.

Many first time buyers walk away at that point, and many others buy without knowing what the word means. Both reactions cost money. A rebuilt title is a specific legal status with a specific history, and it changes the price, the insurance, and the resale value in ways you should understand before you pay.

## What a rebuilt title means

A rebuilt title means an insurance company once declared the car a total loss, and the car was later repaired and passed a state inspection that allowed it to return to the road.

The chain usually works like this. A car is damaged in a crash, a flood, a fire, or a theft recovery. The insurance company decides the cost to repair it is more than the car is worth, or more than a set percentage of its value, often around 70 to 80 percent depending on the state and the insurer. The insurer pays the owner, takes the car, and the state issues a salvage title. A salvage title car cannot be legally driven on public roads.

Someone then buys the salvage car, repairs it, and applies for a rebuilt title. The state inspects the car, usually to confirm it is roadworthy and that stolen parts were not used, and if it passes, the state issues a rebuilt title. The car can then be registered, insured, and driven again.

The key point is that rebuilt is a history label, not a condition grade. It tells you the car was once written off. It does not tell you how badly it was damaged, how well it was repaired, or how safe it is now.

## Why a rebuilt title car costs less

A rebuilt title car should cost much less than the same car with a clean title. A common rule of thumb is 20 to 40 percent less, but the real discount depends on the car, the damage, and your local market. The discount exists for real reasons, and you should not pay clean title money for a rebuilt title car.

First, buyers cannot be sure about the repair quality. A professional shop with photos and receipts is different from a quick fix in a driveway, and most buyers cannot tell the difference from a listing. The market prices in that uncertainty.

Second, insurance can be harder and more expensive. Some insurers will only offer liability coverage, not full coverage, or will pay out less if the car is totaled again. Call your insurer with the VIN before you buy and ask exactly what coverage they will offer and what it will cost.

Third, financing is harder. Many banks and credit unions will not finance a rebuilt title car, or they will lend less against it. If you need a loan, check with your lender first. Many rebuilt title cars are cash purchases for this reason.

Fourth, resale is harder. The next buyer will have the same questions you have now, and the same discount will apply. If you plan to sell the car in a year or two, expect a smaller pool of buyers and a lower price.

These are not reasons to refuse every rebuilt title car. They are reasons the price must reflect them.

## How a car gets a rebuilt title

The details vary by state, but the path is similar. After a total loss, the car gets a salvage title. A rebuilder repairs it, keeps receipts for major parts, and books a state inspection. The inspection checks the VIN, looks for stolen parts, and confirms the car meets basic safety and equipment standards. It is not the same as a full mechanical inspection by your own mechanic.

## What to check before you buy

Do not buy a rebuilt title car on price alone. Work through these checks in order, and be ready to walk away if the seller cannot answer them.

Ask what happened to the car. Was it a crash, a flood, a theft recovery, or something else? Ask when it happened and how long ago. A crash five years ago with 40,000 miles driven since is different from a flood last month. If the seller does not know, or will not say, that is a red flag.

Ask for repair photos and receipts. A good rebuilder keeps before and after photos, a list of parts replaced, and receipts from the shop. Photos of the damage tell you more than any description. If there are no photos and no receipts, you are buying blind, and the price should reflect that.

Check the VIN. Run a vehicle history report and check the NHTSA site for open safety recalls. The history report may show the total loss event, the salvage auction, and the date the rebuilt title was issued. It will not show every repair, but it can confirm the seller's story or contradict it.

Get a pre-purchase inspection from a mechanic you choose, not one the seller recommends. Tell the mechanic the car has a rebuilt title and ask them to check the frame, the alignment, the airbag system, the electrical system, and signs of flood damage such as rust under the seats, silt in crevices, and a musty smell that does not go away. A frame that is bent or poorly straightened is a reason to walk away. Flood damage is also a reason to walk away, because water damage to wiring and electronics can cause problems for years.

Check the title itself. Make sure the name on the title matches the seller's ID, the VIN on the title matches the VIN on the car, and the title says rebuilt, not salvage. A salvage title car cannot be driven legally, and you do not want to be the one to discover that after you pay. Also check for liens. If a lender still has a claim on the car, the title is not clear.

Call your insurer and your lender, if you need one, with the VIN in hand. Ask about coverage, cost, and loan terms before you agree to a price. A car you cannot insure at a price you can afford is not a deal, no matter how low the asking price is.

Finally, test drive it properly. Drive on the highway and on rough roads. Listen for rattles, pulling to one side, and vibrations. Check that all the electronics work, including lights, windows, air conditioning, and safety features.

## When a rebuilt title car can be a good buy

A rebuilt title car can be a good buy in a narrow set of cases. The damage was minor or cosmetic, the repair was done by a reputable shop, the seller has photos and receipts, the car has been driven for a year or more since the repair with no problems, your mechanic finds no frame or flood damage, and the price is well below a clean title car. In that case you are getting a usable car at a real discount, and you are paying for the history label, not for a hidden problem.

It can also work if you plan to keep the car for a long time. The resale discount hurts less if you drive the car for five years instead of one, because you bought it cheap and you got the use out of it. It works less well if you plan to flip it or sell it soon, because the next buyer will want the same discount you got.

## When to walk away

Walk away if the seller cannot explain what happened, has no photos or receipts, or rushes you to decide. Walk away if the inspection finds frame damage, flood damage, airbag problems, or electrical issues. Walk away if your insurer will not cover it the way you need, or your lender will not finance it. Walk away if the price is close to a clean title car, because then you are taking on all the risk with none of the discount. And walk away if the title does not match the seller, the VIN does not match, or anything about the paperwork feels off. A cheap car with bad paperwork is not cheap.

## A short checklist

Use this before you pay for a rebuilt title car. Damage story: the seller can explain what happened and when. Photos and receipts: before and after photos, plus a parts list and shop receipts. History report: total loss event shown, story matches the report. Recalls: VIN checked for open safety recalls. Inspection: your own mechanic checked the frame, airbags, and flood signs, and found no deal breaker. Insurance: your insurer confirmed coverage and cost with the VIN. Financing: your lender confirmed terms, or you are paying cash. Title: rebuilt, not salvage, name matches the seller, VIN matches the car, no lien. Price: well below a clean title car, with room for repairs you might find later. If you cannot check most of these boxes, slow down.
