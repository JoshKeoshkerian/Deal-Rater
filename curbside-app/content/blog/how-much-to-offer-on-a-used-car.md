## Introduction

Buying from a private seller can save you money compared with a dealer price, but it also leaves the pricing up to you. There is no sticker with a market price, no sales manager to negotiate with, and no financing office to hide the real cost. You have to decide what the car is worth, and then convince the seller to agree.

Most first time buyers ask the wrong question. They ask what percentage below the asking price they should offer. There is no good answer to that, because the asking price might be fair, high, or low. The right question is simpler: what are similar cars actually listed for, and what is this specific car worth after you account for its condition, mileage, title, and the work it needs? This post walks through that process in five steps, with a checklist you can use on any private party car.

## There is no magic percentage

A common piece of advice is to offer 10 or 20 percent below the asking price. That advice is useless, because it starts from the wrong number. If a seller lists a car 20 percent above the market, offering 20 percent below asking just gets you to a fair price. If a seller lists a car at a fair price, offering 20 percent below asking is a lowball that most sellers will ignore. And if a seller lists a car below the market because they need a quick sale, offering 20 percent below asking means you overpaid for the privilege of insulting them.

Your offer should come from the market, not from the asking price. That takes more work than picking a percentage, but it is the difference between a good deal and a bad one, and it gives the seller a reason to say yes.

## Step 1: Build your comp set

Start by finding 5 to 8 similar cars for sale by private sellers near you. Similar means the same make and model, the same year or within a year or two, the same trim when you can tell, and mileage within about 20 percent. A 2019 Honda Accord Sport with 120,000 miles is not comparable to a 2019 Accord LX with 60,000 miles, and neither is comparable to a dealer listing.

Use private party listings only. Dealer prices include reconditioning, lot overhead, and a profit margin, so they run higher than private party prices for the same car. If you anchor on dealer prices, almost any private car looks cheap, and you will overpay.

Write down the asking price of each comp. You are looking for a range, not a single number. If most similar cars are listed between 13,000 and 15,000 dollars, that range is your starting point. A car listed at 17,000 dollars needs a clear reason to be worth more, like much lower mileage, a clean one owner history, or new tires and brakes. A car listed at 11,000 dollars also needs a reason, and that reason might be a problem.

## Step 2: Adjust for the differences

Once you have the range, adjust it for how this car differs from the comps. Mileage is usually the biggest factor. A car with 30,000 more miles than the average comp is worth less, even if it looks the same in photos. Condition comes next. Worn tires, thin brakes, stained seats, and faded paint all cost money to fix, and they should show up in your offer.

Title status matters more than most buyers expect. A rebuilt title means the car was declared a total loss and then repaired and inspected. It can be a fair car at the right price, but it should be priced well below a clean title car, and it can be harder to insure and harder to sell later. If the listing does not state the title status, ask before you make an offer.

Service history and owner count are worth something too. A car with full maintenance records and two owners is easier to trust than a car with five owners and no records, even at the same mileage. Ask when the tires, brakes, battery, and fluids were last done, and whether the big services for that mileage were completed.

## Step 3: Subtract the near term costs

Before you make a final offer, get a pre-purchase inspection from a mechanic you choose, at your cost. This is the step that saves buyers the most money, because it turns guesses into numbers. A 150 dollar inspection that finds 900 dollars of worn suspension and a leaking seal is worth far more than it costs.

Take the inspection findings and price them. Use real estimates from a shop, not guesses. Add up the tires, brakes, fluids, and any service that is due soon, like the 100,000 mile service on many cars. Subtract that total from the comp range. That gives you a price that reflects the car in front of you, not an average car.

Also check the VIN for open safety recalls on the NHTSA recall site before you pay. An open recall is not always a deal breaker, but it is something to ask the seller to fix at a dealer before you buy, or to factor into your decision.

## Step 4: Set your walk away number first

Decide the most you will pay before you talk numbers with the seller. This is your walk away number, and it should include everything, not just the price of the car. Add sales tax, title and registration fees, and the first insurance payment. A 14,000 dollar car can easily cost 15,500 dollars out the door, and your budget has to cover the whole amount.

Write the number down. If the seller will not meet it, be ready to walk away and wait for the next car. There is always another listing. The buyers who overpay are usually the ones who decided they had to have this specific car, on this specific day.

## Step 5: Make a specific offer, in writing

Make your offer in writing, by message, so there is a record. Give the number, and give the reasons in two or three short sentences. A specific offer with reasons is much easier for a seller to accept than a round number with no explanation.

Here is a shape that works: "Thanks for showing me the car. Based on five similar Accords listed nearby between 13,000 and 15,000 dollars, and the inspection finding that it needs front brakes and tires, about 900 dollars of work, I can offer 13,200 dollars. I can pay in person this week and handle the title transfer with you."

Notice what that does. It shows the seller you did the work. It ties the number to real comps and real repair costs, not to a feeling. And it makes the sale easy by saying how and when you will pay.

If the seller counters, compare their number to your walk away number, not to your opening offer. If their counter is below your walk away number and the inspection is clean, you have a deal. If it is above, say so plainly and thank them. You can always come back if the car is still listed in a week.

## When the seller says no

A no is not always final. Some sellers need a day to think, and some come back after other buyers fall through. If you liked the car, leave the door open with a short message: "I understand, and my offer stands at 13,200 dollars if you change your mind. Good luck with the sale." Then keep looking.

Do not chase the car by raising your offer above your walk away number. That number existed for a reason. A car you overpaid for does not get better because you wanted it badly.

## Mistakes that cost buyers money

Five mistakes show up again and again. First, anchoring on the asking price or on dealer prices, which makes a high price look normal. Second, offering a round percentage below asking with no reason, which sellers read as a lowball. Third, skipping the inspection and finding the repair bill after you paid. Fourth, forgetting taxes, title, registration, and insurance, and then finding out the total is more than the budget. Fifth, letting urgency set the price, because someone else might buy it. A good car at a fair price can sell fast, but urgency plus a price above your walk away number is how buyers overpay.

## A short checklist before you offer

Use this before you offer. Comps: 5 to 8 similar private party cars nearby, same trim and similar mileage, with a price range written down. Title: status stated, name on the title matches the seller's ID. VIN: shared by the seller, checked for open recalls. Inspection: done by your mechanic, findings priced. Walk away number: set, including tax, title, registration, and insurance. Offer: written, specific, with reasons. If you cannot check most of these boxes, slow down.
