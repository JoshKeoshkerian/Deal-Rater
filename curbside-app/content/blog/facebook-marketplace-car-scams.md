## Introduction

Buying a used car on Marketplace can work well. Many private sellers are honest, and the price is often lower than a dealer price. It can also go wrong fast. A car is usually one of the largest purchases a buyer makes. Marketplace does not vet sellers, verify titles, or protect vehicle payments. The checks fall on you, before you send money. This post covers the red flags that show up again and again in car scams.

## How most scams work

Most scams rely on a good price, a little urgency, and a payment method that is hard to reverse. One pattern is a car listed far below similar cars nearby, with a story that explains the low price. The seller then asks for a deposit to hold the car, often through Zelle, Cash App, Venmo, wire transfer, or a gift card. Once that money is sent, the seller stops replying, or the car never existed. Another pattern uses a real account that was taken over, or a copied dealer page. The listing looks normal, but the seller refuses a meeting, refuses an inspection, or insists on payment before you see the car. A third pattern is a real car with a hidden problem, like a salvage title or a VIN that does not match the car.

## 1. The price is far below similar cars, with no clear reason

Start with the market, not the listing. Search for the same year, make, model, trim, and similar mileage near you, from private sellers. If most similar cars are listed near 12,000 dollars and this one is listed at 5,000 dollars, ask why. A fair reason is specific and checkable: high mileage, cosmetic damage shown in photos, a rebuilt title disclosed in the listing, or missing service records. A weak reason is vague or emotional, and it does not explain the size of the discount. If you cannot find similar cars to compare, be more careful, not less. Accuracy is best in markets with lots of listings.

## 2. The seller asks for money before you see the car

Do not send a deposit to hold a car you have not seen in person. Do not pay in full before you have the title in hand and have checked that the name on the title matches the seller's ID. Be extra careful when the seller asks for payment by Zelle, Cash App, Venmo, wire transfer, crypto, or gift cards, and says it is refundable or safe. Those methods are often hard or impossible to reverse once the money is sent. A normal private sale usually works like this: you message, you ask questions, you meet in a public place in daylight, you test drive, you get an inspection if you want one, you check the title, and then you pay and sign the title over.

## 3. The seller will not meet, talk, or let you inspect the car

A real seller should be willing to meet in person, answer questions, and let you look at the car closely. They should also be willing to let a mechanic inspect it, at your cost, before you buy. Slow down a lot if the seller refuses to meet in person and offers to ship the car, refuses a phone or video call, says the car is in another city and cannot be shown, or refuses a pre-purchase inspection. Meet in a public place in daylight, and bring someone with you when you can.

## 4. The photos, description, and seller profile do not line up

Are there enough photos to see the whole car, including the interior, tires, and damage? Do the photos look like they were taken in the same place, at the same time, of the same car? Be careful when a listing has only one or two photos, stock photos that do not match the description, or a very short or copied description with no detail about this car. Also check the seller profile. A new account with no history, no photo, and several cars listed at low prices is different from a long standing account with a normal mix of items. Search a distinctive sentence from the description in quotes in a search engine. If the same text shows up in other cities, or for a different car, that is a strong warning sign.

## 5. Title problems, and the seller is not the owner on paper

The title is the paper that proves who can sell the car. Ask to see it before you pay. Is the title clean, salvage, or rebuilt? A rebuilt title means the car was declared a total loss and then repaired and inspected, in most states. It can be a fair car at the right price, but it should be priced lower than a clean title car, and it can be harder to insure and harder to sell later. Does the name on the title match the seller's ID? If not, ask why. Is there a lien listed? Does the VIN on the title match the VIN on the car, on the dashboard and the driver door jamb? If the numbers do not match, stop. If the seller cannot show a title, or the story about the title keeps changing, walk away.

## 6. The VIN is missing, wrong, or cannot be checked

The VIN is the car's fingerprint. A seller who will not share it before a meeting is making it hard for you to do basic checks. When you have the VIN, check for open safety recalls on the NHTSA recall site, at nhtsa.gov/recalls. Compare the VIN in the listing to the VIN on the car and on the title, and check that the year, make, model, and trim match the listing. You can also run a vehicle history report, if you choose to buy one, and read it for title brands, reported accidents, and odometer readings. A history report helps, but it is not complete. Not every accident or repair is reported, and a clean report does not mean the car is in good shape.

## 7. Pressure, urgency, and a story that keeps changing

Scams often use time pressure: "Someone else is coming in an hour." "I have three other buyers." "If you do not send a deposit now, I will sell it to the next person." Some pressure is normal in a popular listing. A good car at a fair price can sell fast. But pressure combined with other red flags, like no meeting, no inspection, and payment by wire, is different from a busy seller. Also watch for a story that changes. The seller is moving today, then they are in the military, then a family member is selling it for them. Honest sellers can make a mistake, but a pattern of changing details is a reason to stop and recheck everything.

## What to ask before a test drive

Ask these by message, so you have the answers in writing: How long have you owned the car, and why are you selling it? Is the title clean, and is it in your name? Can I see it when we meet? What is the VIN, and has the car had any accidents, major repairs, or open recalls? Has it had regular maintenance, and do you have records or receipts? Can I have a mechanic inspect it before I buy, and can we meet in a public place in daylight? Also ask about tires, brakes, and any warning lights. A seller who answers plainly and shares records is easier to trust than a seller who avoids detail.

## A short checklist for any listing

Use this before you message, and again before you pay. Price: search 5 to 8 similar private party cars nearby. Is this price in the normal range, or far below it? Photos: are there enough clear photos of the outside, inside, tires, and damage? Description: does it give year, mileage, trim, title status, and known issues, or is it vague and copied? Title: is the title status stated, and will the seller show it in person? VIN: is it listed or shared on request, and does it match the car and title? Seller: does the profile look normal, and does the seller answer questions directly? Meeting: will they meet in public, in daylight, and allow an inspection? Payment: will they accept a normal in person payment, with the title signed over at the same time? If you cannot answer most of those questions with a yes, do not rush.

## What to do if you already sent money

Act fast. Contact your bank or payment app right away, save screenshots of the listing and messages, and report it to Marketplace and to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. Do not send more money to "recover" the first payment.

## The limits of any listing check

No checklist can guarantee a car is good. Asking prices are not sale prices, and a listing cannot tell you how a car drives, how it was maintained, or what will break next. A clean title does not mean a clean car, and a low price does not always mean a scam. That is why an inspection matters, especially for a first car or a car near the top of your budget. A mechanic can check brakes, tires, leaks, rust, suspension, and warning lights in a way photos cannot. Buy the car, not the story. Compare the price to similar private party cars, check the title and VIN, meet in person, and pay only when the paper and the car line up.
