The score

Other deal scores hide their math. This one shows it.

Four readings, each weighted, each visible. You can see exactly which one pulled the number down and by how much — and if you disagree with a weight, you can see that too. A number you can't check is a number you shouldn't trust.

  • Price residual carries half the score — how far the ask sits from what comparable private-party listings suggest, adjusted for mileage and trim.
  • Vehicle risk is scored separately, never folded in. A rebuilt-title car priced low for exactly that reason is a risky car that's fairly priced. One number can't say both things at once.
  • Seller and scam risk stands on its own, so a warning arrives as a warning — not as a few points quietly shaved off a total you'd never notice.
  • Information completeness rewards sellers who actually told you something, and marks down the listings that left you guessing.

Why the comps matter

Marketplace gives you a price and nothing to judge it against.

Most price checkers compare a private-party car to dealer listings — cars carrying reconditioning markup, warranty, and lot overhead. Against that baseline nearly every Marketplace car looks like a steal. Curbside compares it to the only fair benchmark: other private sellers, right now, near you.

Dealer benchmark

"$4,100 below market."

Measured against cars that were detailed, warrantied, and sold off a lot. That gap is the dealer's overhead, not your discount.

Curbside benchmark

"Below what private sellers ask."

Measured against comparable listings from people just like the one you're about to message. Adjusted for mileage and trim.

What it checks

Six readings on every listing.

Kept separate on purpose. A risky car can be fairly priced, and a clean car can be a bad buy.

Price

Is the ask fair?

An expected range built from comparable listings nearby, adjusted for mileage and trim — and how far this one sits outside it.

Scam

Does it smell wrong?

Flags the patterns real scam listings share: far below market with no reason given, thin or templated descriptions, wire-only payment, won't meet in person.

Vehicle

What's wrong with this car?

Open safety recalls, complaint density for this model year, and title flags — salvage, rebuilt, branded — from free federal NHTSA data.

Leverage

How much room do you have?

Days on the market, price drops, and seller wording that signals motivation — then an offer in three moves, and a message you can edit and send.

Inspection

What should you ask?

The known failure modes for this model at this mileage, so you show up asking about the transmission instead of nodding at the paint.

Alternatives

Is there a better one?

Similar cars listed nearby at better prices — because sometimes the honest answer is that the next listing down is the one you want.

What we won't pretend

Two limits, stated up front instead of buried in a terms page.

It's a beta signal, not a rating.

The weights and the discount curve are starting hypotheses. They haven't been checked against a set of hand-evaluated listings yet, and until they are, treat the number as a reason to look closer — not a verdict.

These are asking prices, not sale prices.

Marketplace never shows what a car actually sold for. Every figure here describes how comparable vehicles are advertised. That's a weaker claim than a valuation, and you should know it going in.
